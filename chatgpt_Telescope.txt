Yes. If you provide the right data, I can compute where your telescope is pointing and how to align it to celestial coordinates (Right Ascension/Declination or Altitude/Azimuth).

Here's what I'd need:

GPS
Latitude (°)

UTC date and time (or local time and time zone)
This is essential because the sky changes continuously.
IMU (6-axis)
Accelerometer: Ax, Ay, Az
Gyroscope: Gx, Gy, Gz
Even better if you already have a fused orientation (roll, pitch, yaw) from a complementary or Kalman filter.
Telescope information
How the IMU is mounted relative to the optical tube.
Is it an Alt-Az mount or Equatorial mount?
Any offsets between the IMU and telescope axis.

With that information I can:

Calculate local sidereal time from your GPS and UTC.
Determine the telescope's orientation from the IMU.
Convert the telescope pointing into celestial coordinates (RA/Dec).
Tell you exactly what object you're pointing at.
Calculate the correction needed to point at any target (e.g. Jupiter, Saturn, M31).
Continuously update pointing as new IMU data arrives.

If you're building a GoTo telescope, I can also help implement the full pointing model:

IMU sensor fusion (Madgwick or Mahony filter)
GPS integration
Magnetic declination correction (if using a magnetometer)
Star alignment (1-, 2-, or 3-star)
Alt/Az ↔ RA/Dec coordinate transformations
Motor control corrections for stepper or servo drives

One note: with only a 6-axis IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope), the system does not know its absolute compass heading. Gyroscopes drift over time, so without either:

a 9-axis IMU (adds a magnetometer), or
a star alignment procedure,

the azimuth will gradually become inaccurate. A one- or two-star alignment can eliminate the need for a magnetometer and often gives better astronomical accuracy.

If you're sending live data, I can process each update and continually compute the telescope's pointing direction and the adjustments needed to center your target.

That architecture makes it easy for someone to swap out, say, a different IMU or camera without changing the rest of the system.

I also like your idea of using the Moon as the first alignment target. Many systems require a two- or three-star alignment before they're usable. Your approach could be:

Power on.
Acquire GPS and UTC.
Level the mount with the IMU.
Slew to the predicted Moon position.
Use computer vision to center the Moon automatically.
Compute the pointing model.
From then on, use plate solving on star fields to refine the model and maintain accuracy.

That would be a very user-friendly workflow.

Another idea is to support multiple operating modes:

Beginner Mode: "Find the Moon" → automatic alignment.
Planet Mode: Use planetary image recognition to refine pointing.
Deep Sky Mode: Plate solving for maximum precision.
Research Mode: Expose all sensor data, calibration values, and diagnostics.

This makes the project accessible to both newcomers and experienced telescope builders.
